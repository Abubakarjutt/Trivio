Nobody warned you about the tax thing.

You got your first big client payment, you were thrilled, you spent it — and then someone mentioned "self-employment tax" and "quarterly estimated payments" and suddenly you're googling at midnight trying to figure out if you owe money you don't have.

Here's the plain-English version of what's actually going on.

## You're Paying Two Kinds of Tax

When you were an employee, your employer paid half your Social Security and Medicare taxes for you. As a freelancer, you pay both halves yourself. That's the self-employment tax — currently **15.3%** of your net profit.

On top of that, you still owe regular income tax, which depends on your total income and tax bracket.

So the rough math most freelancers use: **set aside 30–35%** of every payment you receive. That covers both in most situations without requiring you to know your exact bracket in advance.

The good news: you can deduct half of the self-employment tax when you file, which softens it slightly.

## Quarterly Taxes: Why This Exists

Employees have tax withheld from each paycheck automatically. You don't have that. So the IRS requires freelancers (and anyone with significant non-withheld income) to pay estimated taxes four times a year.

**The deadlines:**
- April 15 — for income earned Jan–Mar
- June 15 — for income earned Apr–May
- September 15 — for income earned Jun–Aug
- January 15 — for income earned Sep–Dec

If you don't pay quarterly and owe more than $1,000 when you file, you'll likely owe a small underpayment penalty. It's not the end of the world, but it's annoying and avoidable.

## How Much to Pay Each Quarter

There are two safe ways:

**Option 1 (easiest):** Pay 25% of what you put aside each quarter. If you've been saving 30% of every payment, send 25% of that pile to the IRS four times a year.

**Option 2 (precise):** Pay at least 100% of last year's total tax bill, divided by four. This is called the "safe harbor" method — as long as you hit it, you won't owe a penalty even if you end up owing more at filing time.

Most freelancers use Option 1 in their first year and switch to Option 2 once they have a year of history to reference.

## What Counts as Income

Everything a client pays you counts — whether it's a bank transfer, PayPal, Venmo, cash, or a check. If a US client pays you more than $600 in a year, they're required to send you (and the IRS) a 1099-NEC form. But even if they don't send one, you still owe tax on the income.

Income from platforms like Upwork, Fiverr, and Toptal is also taxable. They may send you a 1099-K if you exceed certain thresholds, but again — you owe tax on it either way.

## What Reduces Your Tax Bill

Every legitimate business expense reduces your taxable income. Common ones freelancers miss:

- **Home office deduction** — if you work from a dedicated space at home, you can deduct a portion of rent/mortgage, utilities, and internet proportional to the square footage
- **Health insurance premiums** — if you pay your own, these are often fully deductible
- **Software subscriptions** — every tool you use for work (Figma, Adobe, GitHub, Notion, Zoom, etc.)
- **Equipment** — laptop, monitor, microphone, camera
- **Professional development** — courses, books, conferences
- **Phone** — the business-use portion of your phone bill

Keep receipts. The standard is "ordinary and necessary" for your business. When in doubt, track it and let your accountant make the call at filing time.

## The Simplest System

1. Open a savings account just for taxes
2. Every time you get paid, move 30% into it immediately
3. On each quarterly deadline, send a payment from that account
4. File your return by April 15 and either pay the remainder or receive a refund

That's it. You don't need to understand every corner of the tax code to stay out of trouble — you just need a consistent habit of setting the money aside before you can spend it.
