Tax deductions are one of the few genuine perks of being self-employed. As a freelancer, you can subtract many of your business expenses from your taxable income before calculating what you owe — which can make a significant difference in your tax bill.

Most freelancers miss at least a few. Here's the complete picture.

## How Deductions Work

When you earn $60,000 in freelance income and have $10,000 in deductible business expenses, you only pay income tax on $50,000. You still owe self-employment tax on your net profit, but deductions reduce both.

The rule for what qualifies: the expense must be **ordinary** (common in your field) and **necessary** (helpful for your business). You don't need receipts for expenses under $75 in the US, but keeping them anyway is good practice.

## Software and Subscriptions

Every tool you use for work is deductible:

- Design: Figma, Adobe Creative Cloud, Sketch, Canva Pro
- Development: GitHub, JetBrains, AWS, Vercel, Netlify, DigitalOcean
- Writing: Grammarly, Notion, Ulysses
- Communication: Zoom, Slack, Loom
- AI tools: ChatGPT Plus, Claude Pro, GitHub Copilot, Midjourney
- Project management: Linear, Asana, ClickUp, Basecamp
- File storage: Dropbox, Google Workspace, iCloud for work

If you use a subscription for both personal and work purposes, you can deduct the business-use portion (e.g., 80% of a cloud storage plan you use mostly for work files).

## Equipment and Hardware

- Laptop or desktop computer
- External monitor(s)
- Keyboard, mouse, webcam, headphones, microphone
- Phone (the business-use percentage)
- Tablet (if used for work)
- External hard drives, USB hubs
- Desk, ergonomic chair (if purchased specifically for work)

For expensive items, you may need to depreciate them over several years, or you may be able to deduct the full cost in the year of purchase under Section 179 (US) or similar rules. Check with a local accountant for the rules in your country.

## Home Office Deduction

If you have a space in your home used **regularly and exclusively** for work, you can deduct a portion of your housing costs.

The percentage is based on square footage: if your home office is 10% of your home's total area, you can deduct 10% of:
- Rent or mortgage interest
- Electricity and gas
- Internet (though this is usually fully deductible as a direct expense)
- Renters or homeowners insurance
- General repairs and maintenance

**In the US**, there's also a simplified method: $5 per square foot of office space, up to 300 sq ft ($1,500 maximum). Less precise, but much easier to calculate.

The "exclusive use" rule is strict — a corner of your bedroom with a desk technically doesn't qualify if it's also your bedroom. A dedicated room or clearly partitioned space does.

## Internet and Phone

- **Internet:** Usually 100% deductible if you work from home and it's your primary connection. If you use it for both work and personal use, deduct the work-use percentage (typically 80–90% for remote freelancers).
- **Phone:** Deduct the business-use percentage. If you use your phone 50% for work calls and client communication, deduct 50% of your monthly bill.

## Professional Development

- Online courses (Udemy, Coursera, Skillshare, Domestika)
- Books and ebooks related to your work
- Conference tickets and registration fees
- Workshops and seminars

Learning that maintains or improves skills in your current field is deductible. A graphic designer taking a new Figma course: yes. The same designer taking a cooking class: no.

## Health Insurance

If you're self-employed and pay your own health insurance premiums (not through a spouse's plan), you can often deduct 100% of those premiums as a business deduction. This is one of the biggest tax breaks available to freelancers and is frequently missed.

Check with a local tax professional — the exact rules depend on your country and situation.

## Business Travel

When you travel **primarily for business purposes**:

- Flights and trains
- Hotel or Airbnb
- Car rental
- Taxis and rideshare

A working trip that's 60% business and 40% vacation can still have the business portion deducted. Keep a log of which days were work-related.

## Meals and Entertainment

In the US, business meals are 50% deductible when you're with a client, prospect, or business partner and discussing business. "Grabbing lunch to catch up" probably doesn't qualify. "Meeting a potential client to discuss a project" does.

Keep a note of who you were with and what was discussed. A quick note in your expense app at the time is enough.

## Retirement Contributions

Freelancers can contribute to retirement accounts that reduce taxable income:

- **SEP-IRA (US):** Contribute up to 25% of net self-employment income (max ~$69,000 for 2024). These contributions are fully deductible.
- **Solo 401(k):** Even more flexible contribution limits.
- **RRSP (Canada):** Similar benefits for Canadian freelancers.

This is the tax deduction most freelancers leave on the table the longest. Contributing $10,000 to a SEP-IRA doesn't just help future-you — it reduces your current tax bill by $10,000 times your marginal rate.

## What to Track

For every business expense:
- The amount
- The date
- What it was for
- Why it was for business

A simple spreadsheet or an accounting app that lets you categorize transactions as you go is enough. The goal is to have a clear record if you're ever audited — not a perfect accounting system.

Track everything. Let your tax professional make the call on what's deductible. The cost of missing a deduction is always greater than the cost of tracking something that turns out not to qualify.
