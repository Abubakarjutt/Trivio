You finished a big project. The client paid. Life is good.

Then the end of the month hits and you have no idea where that money went.

Sound familiar? You're not alone. Most freelancers go through the same cycle: work comes in, money comes in, and then somehow, it's just... gone. Not on anything crazy. Not on a vacation. Just gone.

Here's why that happens — and what you can do about it without becoming an accountant.

## The Real Problem: Freelance Money Moves Weirdly

A regular employee gets paid the same amount, on the same date, every two weeks. Tax is already taken out. The math is almost done for them.

You don't get that.

Your money arrives in lumps. One month it's $8,000. The next it's $1,200. A client pays 60 days late. Another pays in two installments. And all of it is pre-tax — meaning somewhere between 25% and 40% of every payment isn't really yours.

This creates a trap: you see a big number land in your account, you spend like you earned it, and then three months later you're scrambling before a tax deadline.

## Step 1: Separate Your Money (This Is Non-Negotiable)

Open a second bank account. Right now, if you haven't already.

Everything a client pays you goes into the business account. What you actually live on comes out of it as a "salary" you pay yourself.

This one change makes tracking trivially easy because you're not trying to filter personal Amazon orders out of your business expenses.

## Step 2: Set Aside Tax Money Every Single Payment

Every time a client pays you, move 30% of it to a savings account. Don't touch it.

Yes, 30% might be more than you'll actually owe. That's fine — you'll get the difference back when you file. The point is to never be surprised.

If you're in the US and making more than $1,000/quarter in profit, you're required to pay estimated quarterly taxes anyway. Set a phone reminder: April 15, June 15, September 15, January 15.

## Step 3: Track Expenses as They Happen — Not at Tax Time

Here's the thing about trying to reconstruct expenses after the fact: you will forget 30% of them. That's money you paid taxes on that you didn't have to.

The expenses that actually add up for freelancers:
- Software subscriptions (Figma, Notion, Slack, Adobe, GitHub)
- Equipment (laptop, monitor, headphones, phone)
- Home office (a portion of your rent/mortgage and utilities if you work from home)
- Professional development (courses, books, conferences)
- Client meals and entertainment (50% deductible in the US)
- Health insurance premiums (often fully deductible for self-employed people)

The simplest system: when you buy something for work, take five seconds to mark it. Whether that's a note in your phone, a receipt photo, or a quick entry in a bookkeeping app. Five seconds now saves two hours in April.

## Step 4: Know Your Numbers (Just Three of Them)

You don't need to understand double-entry accounting. You need to know:

**1. What came in this month** — total client payments received.

**2. What went out this month** — business expenses only, not your personal salary.

**3. Your average monthly revenue over the last 6 months** — this is your real income. Not the good months. The average.

That last number is the one most freelancers ignore. If your average is $4,500/month, plan your life around $4,500/month. Not around your $9,000 January.

## Step 5: Invoice Immediately and Follow Up Without Embarrassment

Half of "where did my money go" is actually "where is money that I'm owed but haven't collected."

Send invoices the same day you finish work, or the day a milestone is hit. Don't wait until the end of the month. Don't wait until you feel like the client is ready. Send it.

If it's unpaid after 14 days, send a one-line reminder with no apology: "Just following up on Invoice #42, due [date]. Let me know if you have any questions." 

Outstanding invoices are money you've already earned. Following up isn't awkward — it's just accounting.

## The Tools That Actually Help

**For the truly minimal approach:** A spreadsheet with three columns — Date, Description, Amount — split into two tabs (income and expenses). Update it weekly. This works if you're consistent.

**For something that does more of the work:** An accounting app that lets you import your bank statement and categorize transactions automatically. The good ones will flag patterns ("you spent $340 on subscriptions this month"), and let you send invoices from the same place you track expenses.

The goal isn't perfect accounting. The goal is knowing, within a few minutes, what came in, what went out, and roughly what you owe in taxes.

## The Pattern That Actually Works

Spend 15 minutes every Friday doing this:

1. Log any transactions you haven't recorded
2. Send any invoices you've been putting off
3. Chase any invoices that are overdue

That's it. 15 minutes a week keeps you out of the "I have no idea where my money went" spiral.

You don't need to love bookkeeping. You just need a system small enough that you'll actually stick to it.
