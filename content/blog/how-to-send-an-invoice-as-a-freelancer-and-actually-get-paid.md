Getting paid should be the easy part. You did the work — now someone owes you money. But a surprising number of freelancers find invoicing more stressful than the work itself.

Either they're not sure what to include, they feel weird asking for money, or they've had invoices ignored for weeks. This is the guide that fixes all three.

## What Every Invoice Needs

A professional invoice isn't complicated. It needs:

**Your information:**
- Your name (or business name)
- Your address or city/country
- Your email
- Your tax ID or business registration number (where required)

**Client information:**
- Their company name
- Their billing contact name and email
- Their address (especially if you're in different countries)

**Invoice details:**
- A unique invoice number (Invoice #001, #002, etc.)
- Invoice date
- Due date
- A line-by-line breakdown of what you're charging for
- The total amount owed
- Your payment details (bank account, PayPal, etc.)

That's the whole list. You don't need fancy software for this — a PDF template works fine. But having a tool that generates them automatically saves time and makes you look more professional.

## When to Send It

**The answer: immediately.**

Don't wait until the end of the month. Don't wait until you "feel like" the project is settled. Don't wait until you think the client is ready.

Send the invoice the day you finish the work, or the day a milestone is hit. The sooner it arrives, the sooner it enters their payment queue — and most companies process invoices in the order they receive them.

If you bill hourly, send invoices at regular intervals (weekly or bi-weekly) rather than letting them accumulate. A $500 weekly invoice is easier for a client to approve quickly than a $4,000 monthly one that needs a manager's sign-off.

## Payment Terms: What to Write

"Net 30" means they have 30 days to pay. "Net 15" is 15 days. "Due on receipt" means immediately.

For most freelancers working with small businesses, **Net 15 or Net 14** is reasonable and faster than the Net 30 that larger companies sometimes try to impose.

For new clients or large projects, asking for a deposit upfront (25–50%) before work begins is completely normal. It protects you and filters out clients who aren't serious.

## How to Follow Up Without Feeling Awkward

The invoice is overdue. You feel weird bringing it up. You don't want to seem pushy or damage the relationship.

Here's the reframe: **you already did the work**. The awkward situation isn't you asking for money — it's the client not paying what they owe. Following up is just accounting.

A good follow-up sequence:

**Day 1 after due date:**
> "Hi [Name], just a quick note — Invoice #042 for $X was due [date]. Please let me know if you need me to resend it or if there's anything holding up payment."

**Day 7:**
> "Following up again on Invoice #042 ($X). Happy to help with any questions about it — otherwise, could you give me an ETA on payment?"

**Day 14:**
> "Invoice #042 is now [N] days past due. I'll need to pause work on any ongoing projects until this is cleared. Please advise."

No apologies. No softening language like "I'm so sorry to bother you." You did the work; they owe the money. Polite firmness is professional, not rude.

## Late Fees

You can add a late fee clause to your invoices: "Invoices unpaid after 30 days are subject to a 1.5% monthly late fee." Most clients won't pay it, but it creates a paper trail and sometimes accelerates payment just by existing.

More practically: stop doing new work for clients with unpaid invoices past 45 days. Your time is your only asset. Protect it.

## The Fastest Way to Get Paid

1. Send the invoice immediately when work is done
2. Make payment easy — include a direct payment link if possible
3. Use short payment terms (Net 14 or Net 15)
4. Follow up the day after the due date, not weeks later
5. For new clients, take a deposit

The freelancers who get paid fastest aren't the ones who are most persistent — they're the ones with the clearest, simplest invoicing process that leaves no room for confusion or delay.
